World-record half-lives
248 reigns, nine events, Kaplan-Meier and Cox

Survival analysis on athletics world records

Half of all world records fall within 1.5 years

A world record lives from the day it is set to the day it is broken. Measured that way across 248 reigns in nine events, the median record lasts 1.5 years, records set since 1980 fall at about half the rate of older ones, and of the nine standing today the 2023 marathon record is the most likely to go in the next five years (60%).

The curve

Most records fall early

The curve below is the share of records still standing after a given number of years. It drops fast: half are gone by 1.5 years, three quarters by about five. A new record is most exposed right after it is set, while the athlete who set it and the rivals chasing it are still competing. Records that get through the first few years flatten into a long tail, and every record people call untouchable lives in that tail.

Drag the slider, or hover the chart, to set how long a record has already stood. The shaded band is the next five years, and the readout is the chance the record falls inside it: 1 − S(t+5) / S(t).

Why not average the reigns?

An average of past reigns only sees records that have already been broken. It leaves out every record still standing, and those are the long-lived ones, so it comes out short by construction.

Survival analysis treats a standing record as censored: it has lasted at least t years and nobody knows how much longer it will go. Kaplan-Meier keeps it in the risk set for every year it has survived. Of the 248 reigns, 239 ended in a new record and 9 are censored.

Cox model

Two things predict a long reign

The Cox proportional-hazards model was fit on the 239 reigns that have a previous record to compare against; the first record in each event has no improvement margin. A hazard ratio below 1 means a record falls more slowly.

The era effect matches what happened to the sport after 1980: it turned professional, the easy gains were gone, and each new mark got harder to beat. Use the Split at 1980 toggle above to see it as two curves: the median reign is about 1.2 years for the 170 earlier records and about 2.0 years for the 78 later ones. The margin effect is the Beamon case. A record that beats its predecessor by a wide gap leaves the rest of the field further behind.

The data

Nine events passed the completeness gate

Progressions come from Wikipedia's record-progression pages via pandas.read_html. Those pages mix the progression with all-time lists and navigation tables, and a naive scrape can stop an event's history decades early without any visible error. So fetch_data.py keeps an event only if its reconstructed running best is within 0.5% of the ratified record, its history reaches back to 1970 or earlier, and it has at least 12 marks. Nine of 21 candidate events pass.

Every progression decelerates, each new record taking off less than the one before. I fitted asymptotic curves to them (in the static figure), but for events still improving the limit runs off wherever the optimizer lets it, so no event limit is reported and the ranking above uses the survival curve alone.

Limitations

What this doesn't show

The model knows when records fell. It doesn't know the pressure on them now, such as how many athletes are within 1% this season. That would be the next feature to add.

The 0% for the women's 800 m is partly mechanical. No reign in the data ends between 43 and 48 years, so the curve is flat across that stretch and the formula returns zero. Set the slider to 42.9 years to see it. "Very unlikely" is a fair reading; "impossible" is not.

The same event contributes only three reigns, one of them running 55 years from 1925 to 1980, and its 1925 mark (1:54.0) is within a second of the 1980 one. That looks like missing or misread rows. The gate checks the end point and the start year, not the gaps in between.

Sample
248 reigns (239 broken, 9 standing) across nine events, first record 1896
Models
Kaplan-Meier survival; Cox proportional hazards on era, improvement margin and event type
