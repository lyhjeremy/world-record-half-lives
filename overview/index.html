World-record half-lives
248 reigns, nine events, Kaplan-Meier and Cox

Survival analysis on athletics world records

Half of all world records fall within 1.5 years

I treated each world record as a survival time: it starts the day it is set and ends the day it is broken. The nine records still standing are right-censored. On 248 reigns across nine events, a Kaplan-Meier curve gives the median reign, a Cox proportional-hazards model says what makes a record last, and the fitted curve gives each current record a probability of falling in the next five years. The 2023 marathon record comes out at 60%. Kratochvílová's 800 m from 1983 comes out at 0%.

The framing

Why an average reign gives the wrong answer

The quick approach is to average how long past records lasted. That average only sees records that have already been broken, so it leaves out every record still standing, and those are the long-lived ones. The estimate comes out too short by construction.

Survival analysis handles this with censoring. A standing record has lasted at least t years and we don't know how much longer it will go. Kaplan-Meier keeps that partial information in the risk set instead of throwing the record away. Of the 248 reigns here, 239 ended in a new record and 9 are censored.

The curve

Most records fall early

1.5 yr

Kaplan-Meier median survival of a world record, nine events, 1896 to 2026.

The hazard is front-loaded. A new record is most exposed right after it is set, often because the athlete who set it, and the rivals chasing it, are still competing. Records that get through the first few years flatten out into a long tail.

Split by era, the median reign is about 1.2 years for the 170 records set before 1980 and about 2.0 years for the 78 set in 1980 or later. The famous old records are the survivors of that early window, which is why they look so durable now.

Cox model

Two things predict a long reign

The Cox proportional-hazards model was fit on the 239 reigns that have a previous record to compare against (the first record in each event has no improvement margin). Hazard ratios below 1 mean a record falls more slowly.

The era effect fits what happened to the sport after 1980: it turned professional, the easy gains were taken, and each new mark got harder to beat. The margin effect is the Beamon case. A record that beats its predecessor by a wide gap leaves the rest of the field further behind.

The data

Nine events passed the completeness gate

Progressions come from Wikipedia's record-progression pages via pandas.read_html. Those pages mix the real progression with all-time lists and navigation tables, and a naive scrape can stop an event's history decades early without any visible error.

So fetch_data.py keeps an event only if three things hold: its reconstructed running best is within 0.5% of the current ratified record, its history reaches back to 1970 or earlier, and it has at least 12 marks. Nine of the 21 candidate events pass: the men's 100 m, 800 m, 1500 m, 5000 m, 10,000 m, marathon and shot put, and the women's 800 m and high jump. The rest were dropped.

Limitations

What this doesn't show

The model knows when records fell. It doesn't know the current pressure on them, such as how many athletes are within 1% this season, which would be the next feature to add.

The 0% for the women's 800 m is partly mechanical. No reign in the data ends between 43 and 48 years, so the curve is flat over that stretch and the formula returns zero. The same event also contributes only three reigns, one of them running 55 years from 1925 to 1980. That looks like missing rows the gate can't catch, since it checks the end point and the start year, not the gaps in between.

The fitted progression curves are not used to estimate event limits. For events that are still improving, the asymptote isn't identifiable from the data, so the vulnerability ranking relies on the survival curve alone.
